# Tutorial diagnostic model

Read this file after `SKILL.md` has inferred that the intended documentation
reader primarily needs to act while acquiring new competence: the reader is
learning through guided activity.

Use this as diagnostic guidance for reviewing a page. It is not a complete
course-design or tutorial-writing methodology.

## Reader situation

A tutorial serves a learner who:

- is acquiring skills, knowledge, familiarity, or confidence;
- cannot yet be expected to make all the decisions of a competent
  practitioner;
- benefits from a deliberately managed path;
- learns by doing meaningful things and observing what happens.

The practical artifact or result produced during a tutorial is a vehicle for
learning.

It is not necessarily what the learner ultimately needs to learn.

A tutorial is a lesson, not merely an easy or beginner-level how-to.

## Expected content architecture

A good tutorial creates a coherent learning experience.

It should:

- lead the learner through meaningful concrete activity;
- give the learner an achievable outcome to work toward;
- make the path logical and successfully completable;
- expose the learner to the actions, concepts, and tools needed for the
  intended learning;
- show visible, understandable results early and often;
- tell the learner what important results to expect;
- point out things the learner may otherwise fail to notice;
- move from concrete action and observation toward competence;
- minimize explanation while the learner is focused on doing;
- remove unnecessary options and alternatives;
- aim for very high reliability.

The tutorial author carries substantial responsibility for the learning path.

The learner should mostly need to follow the guidance attentively rather than
design the path themselves.

## Learning outcome versus task outcome

Distinguish what the learner does from what the learner is meant to acquire.

A learner may:

- deploy an application;
- configure a database;
- create a project;
- call an API.

But the educational value may be familiarity with:

- the workflow;
- important tools;
- relationships between components;
- terminology;
- cause and effect;
- recurring patterns of practice.

Do not judge the tutorial only by whether the final artifact works.

Ask whether the experience creates the intended competence.

## Diagnostic questions

Ask:

- What should the learner become more capable of after this experience?
- What does the learner do in order to acquire that competence?
- Is there a meaningful achievable outcome from the beginning?
- Does the learner know what they are building toward?
- Does every major step contribute to a coherent learning journey?
- Do important actions produce observable results?
- Can the learner tell whether those results are correct?
- Are expected outputs or states shown where needed?
- Are important observations pointed out?
- Does the sequence make sense for someone encountering this material for the
  first time?
- Is the learner asked to make decisions they are not yet equipped to make?
- Are unnecessary alternatives introduced?
- Does extended explanation interrupt the concrete activity?
- Can the learner reasonably succeed by following the tutorial as written?

## Common failure patterns

### How-to mislabeled as tutorial

The page solves a practical problem for someone who already knows what they are
doing.

It may be simple or introductory, but its purpose is work rather than learning.

### Information dump disguised as teaching

The page explains concepts, lists facts, or describes the system while giving
the learner little meaningful activity.

A tutorial creates conditions for learning through experience rather than
trying to transfer understanding mainly through exposition.

### Too many choices

The learner is repeatedly asked to choose between tools, architectures,
configurations, or optional paths before they have enough competence to make
those decisions confidently.

A tutorial should normally choose a workable path and guide the learner to
success.

### Missing expected results

The learner performs actions but cannot tell whether they worked.

This weakens confidence and breaks the connection between action and effect.

### Explanation interrupts the learning flow

Long conceptual discussions appear while the learner is trying to complete an
action and observe its result.

A brief reason may help orientation.

Deeper explanation should normally be linked rather than inserted into the
learning path.

### Responsibility is delegated to the learner

Instructions assume the learner can troubleshoot gaps, infer missing steps, or
make domain decisions without sufficient experience.

A tutorial must carry more responsibility than a how-to guide.

### Successful task, weak learning experience

The learner produces the expected artifact, but the path does not create useful
familiarity with the actions, concepts, or relationships the tutorial was
supposed to teach.

## Supporting content and boundaries

Use only enough reference and explanation to keep the learner oriented and
moving.

Useful supporting material can include:

- the name of something being used;
- a brief reason for an action;
- the exact value required for the chosen path;
- a warning about a likely mistake;
- expected output;
- something specific the learner should notice.

Avoid turning the tutorial into:

- a catalogue of options;
- a conceptual essay;
- a comprehensive reference page.

## Recommendation patterns

Prefer recommendations that improve the learning experience.

Depending on the diagnosis, recommend one high-value change such as:

- establish a concrete achievable outcome;
- clarify what competence the experience is meant to develop;
- simplify the path to one reliable route;
- add expected results after important actions;
- add cues about what the learner should notice;
- remove premature alternatives and decisions;
- replace abstract exposition with concrete activity;
- move extended explanation elsewhere;
- fill gaps the learner cannot reasonably bridge;
- reorder activities so the learning journey makes sense;
- narrow the tutorial to an experience that can be made reliably successful.

Do not judge a tutorial primarily by whether it solves a useful production
task.

Judge whether the guided activity creates a coherent and successful learning
experience.
