# Explanation diagnostic model

Read this file after `SKILL.md` has inferred that the intended documentation
reader primarily needs cognition while acquiring or deepening understanding:
the reader wants to make sense of a subject rather than complete an immediate
task.

Use this as diagnostic guidance for reviewing a page. It is not a complete
methodology for writing conceptual documentation.

## Reader situation

Explanation serves a reader who:

- wants to understand a concept, system, decision, relationship, or broader
  subject;
- is reflecting rather than solving an urgent task;
- benefits from context, connections, reasons, implications, perspectives, and
  alternatives;
- may read the material away from the product or immediate work.

Useful prompts for explanation often resemble:

> Why is this so?

or:

> Tell me about this.

## Expected content architecture

Good explanation:

- deepens and broadens understanding;
- makes connections between ideas;
- provides background and context;
- explains reasons, design decisions, historical causes, or technical
  constraints;
- draws implications;
- shows relationships with other concepts;
- can discuss alternatives, counterexamples, different approaches, and
  perspectives;
- helps reveal what is otherwise implicit or difficult to see;
- treats a reasonably bounded topic rather than an immediate task.

Explanation is allowed to be discursive.

Unlike reference, it is not restricted to neutral description.

## Structure around understanding

The organizing unit is a meaningful topic, conceptual problem, or question.

Unlike a how-to, explanation does not need to follow the sequence of the
reader's work.

Unlike reference, it does not need to mirror the structure of the machinery.

Instead, the structure should help the reader build a coherent mental model.

Useful organizing relationships can include:

- causes;
- consequences;
- contrasts;
- dependencies;
- alternatives;
- historical development;
- trade-offs;
- conceptual layers;
- different perspectives.

The main structural risk is uncontrolled scope.

Because explanation naturally connects things, it can expand indefinitely
unless the topic is deliberately bounded.

## Diagnostic questions

Ask:

- What is the reader trying to understand?
- What implicit or explicit "why?" or "how does this fit together?" question
  gives the page its purpose?
- Is the topic bounded clearly enough to form one coherent discussion?
- Does the page deepen understanding rather than merely list facts?
- Does it make meaningful connections between ideas?
- Does it provide relevant causes, reasons, context, or implications?
- Where useful, does it consider alternatives or different perspectives?
- Is the organization driven by understanding rather than by a task sequence?
- Is it organized around concepts and relationships rather than merely
  following a product feature tree?
- Has the discussion expanded into loosely related subjects without a useful
  boundary?
- Are substantial instructions embedded in the explanation?
- Has factual description grown large enough to function as separate reference
  material?

## Common failure patterns

### Reference disguised as explanation

The page mostly enumerates facts, fields, components, options, or system
behavior without showing relationships or significance.

The reader receives information but little additional understanding.

### How-to embedded in explanation

A conceptual discussion turns into step-by-step instructions for carrying out
a real task.

The procedure serves a more immediate reader need and may interrupt reflection.

### Unbounded topic

The author keeps adding related concepts because each connection appears
relevant.

The result lacks a coherent center and becomes difficult to understand as one
piece.

### Missing connections

The page defines concepts individually but does not show how they relate to one
another or to the broader subject.

### Context without purpose

History, background, examples, alternatives, and observations accumulate
without helping answer a meaningful conceptual question.

Explanation can be broad.

Its breadth still needs to serve understanding.

### Machinery-led organization

The page follows menus, API objects, modules, or feature categories even though
the reader's need is conceptual.

That structure may work for reference but can obscure the relationships the
explanation exists to reveal.

## Supporting content and boundaries

Explanation can contain facts and short practical examples when they illuminate
the topic.

It can mention tasks to show why a concept matters.

These become problematic when the page begins to serve the immediate work of
performing those tasks or becomes a lookup catalogue.

Explanation itself also needs a boundary.

Connections are valuable only while they contribute to understanding the topic
the page has chosen to address.

## Recommendation patterns

Prefer recommendations that strengthen the reader's mental model.

Depending on the diagnosis, recommend one high-value change such as:

- define the conceptual question more clearly;
- narrow an explanation that has expanded too far;
- reorganize around causes, relationships, implications, or concepts rather
  than system components;
- add missing context that explains why something is the way it is;
- connect isolated concepts that the reader needs to understand together;
- move substantial procedures into how-to guides;
- move detailed factual catalogues into reference;
- separate a related but independently meaningful conceptual question into its
  own explanation page.

Do not strip explanation down to neutral facts in the name of concision.

Discussion, context, connections, perspective, and alternatives are part of
what make explanation useful.
