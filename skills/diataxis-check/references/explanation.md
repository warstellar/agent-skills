# Explanation diagnostic model

Use this reference when the intended reader needs theoretical or propositional
knowledge while acquiring or deepening understanding: the reader is at study
and has stepped back from immediate work to think about a subject.

Explanation develops understanding.

It is neither a task guide nor a factual lookup catalogue.

## Establish the understanding need

Ask what the reader is trying to understand.

Useful underlying questions often resemble:

- Why does this work this way?
- Why was this designed this way?
- How do these concepts relate?
- What are the consequences of this design?
- What alternatives exist?
- How does this fit into the bigger picture?

Do not require every explanation to contain the literal word "why."

The important point is that the reader wants a richer mental model rather than
an immediate action or isolated fact.

## Use work versus study to distinguish explanation from reference

Reference and explanation both concern cognition.

The strongest distinction is the reader situation.

Reference:

> I am working and need information I can apply now.

Explanation:

> I have stepped back from the immediate work and want to understand this
> subject better.

Do not classify theoretical content solely from topic or prose style.

Lists, tables, long paragraphs, and conversational tone are only surface
signals.

When the boundary is doubtful, use the work-versus-study relationship.

## Give the page a bounded conceptual subject

Explanation does not inherit its boundaries as naturally as:

- a how-to guide inherits a task;
- a tutorial inherits a learning journey;
- reference inherits the machinery it describes.

Check whether the page has a coherent conceptual center.

A real or implicit "why?" question can provide one.

An "About X" formulation can also be a useful heuristic for whether the page
has a topic suitable for discussion.

Do not treat either heuristic as a mandatory title format.

The diagnostic question is whether the discussion has a meaningful boundary.

## Watch for uncontrolled scope

Explanation naturally makes connections, so related material can expand
indefinitely.

Do not flag a connection merely because it reaches outside the immediate
topic.

Flag expansion when the new material no longer helps answer the conceptual
question or deepen understanding of the bounded subject.

Broad perspective is compatible with explanation.

Unlimited relatedness is not.

## Look for connections, not just facts

A page does not become explanation merely because it contains conceptual or
technical information.

Check whether it helps the reader understand relationships such as:

- cause and effect;
- dependencies;
- conceptual relationships;
- design consequences;
- historical development;
- trade-offs;
- alternative approaches;
- interactions between parts of a system.

A collection of isolated facts can still be reference-like even when the facts
are conceptual.

Explanation should connect or interpret them.

## Use context when it changes understanding

Appropriate explanatory material can include:

- design reasons;
- historical reasons;
- technical constraints;
- background;
- implications;
- analogies;
- internal mechanisms;
- the bigger picture.

Do not require all of these.

Ask whether the context materially helps the reader understand why the subject
is the way it is or how it fits together.

Background that does not contribute to the conceptual purpose is still
unnecessary.

## Allow perspective and alternatives

Explanation does not have the same neutrality requirement as reference.

Opinion, judgement, and perspective can be legitimate when they help explain
the subject.

Alternatives, counterexamples, competing approaches, and contrary views can
also be useful when they reveal:

- trade-offs;
- limits;
- design choices;
- different ways to interpret the problem.

Do not flag evaluative language merely because it is not neutral.

Instead ask whether it contributes to understanding and whether the page makes
the basis of the judgement sufficiently clear.

## Do not turn explanation into instruction

A practical example can illuminate an explanation.

A complete task procedure serves another reader need.

Check whether the page begins telling the reader how to carry out an
independent real-world task.

Related subject matter does not make the procedure explanatory.

For example, an explanation of authentication architecture does not need to
contain the complete key-rotation procedure merely because key rotation uses
that architecture.

Link to task guidance when the procedure is independently useful.

## Do not turn explanation into reference

Technical facts can support explanation.

A systematic catalogue of fields, commands, options, or properties serves
lookup instead.

Check whether descriptive material has grown large enough that a working reader
would reasonably want to consult it independently.

If so, reference may be the better home for that material.

## Treat embedded explanation proportionally

Other documentation modes can contain short explanatory passages.

Do not conclude that every "why" sentence needs its own explanation page.

The problem arises when an explanatory need is large enough that:

- it interrupts another mode;
- it appears repeatedly in fragments;
- it cannot develop the relationships or reasoning the subject needs;
- readers would benefit from approaching it independently for understanding.

A fragmented explanation can fail both the containing page and the
understanding need.

## Do not require immediate actionability

Explanation can be valuable even when the reader will not apply it in the next
task.

Its effect can be indirect:

- a richer conceptual model;
- better judgement;
- clearer understanding of relationships;
- a changed perspective on the craft.

Do not criticize valid explanation simply because it does not produce an
immediate action.

## Diagnostic questions

Ask:

- What is the reader trying to understand?
- What conceptual question gives the page its center?
- Is the topic bounded enough to form one coherent discussion?
- Does the page make relationships visible rather than merely list facts?
- Does it explain relevant causes, reasons, or constraints?
- Does context materially deepen understanding?
- Would alternatives or counterexamples clarify an important trade-off or
  limit?
- Is the organization driven by understanding rather than by a task sequence?
- Is it following product components when the conceptual model needs a
  different structure?
- Has the topic expanded merely because adjacent subjects are related?
- Has a practical procedure grown into an independent task?
- Has technical description grown into lookup-oriented reference?
- Would the reader naturally use this while actively working, or after
  stepping back to reflect?

## Common structural failures

### Reference disguised as explanation

The page defines or lists concepts and facts but does not develop their
relationships, reasons, or significance.

### How-to embedded in explanation

A conceptual discussion becomes a procedure for accomplishing a real task.

### Unbounded topic

Related material continues to accumulate without one conceptual question or
subject holding the discussion together.

### Missing connections

The page defines several concepts but leaves the reader to infer how they fit
together.

### Context without purpose

History, examples, background, or alternatives appear without deepening the
reader's understanding of the chosen subject.

### Machinery-led conceptual structure

The page follows menus, modules, objects, or feature categories even when the
reader needs relationships that cut across those boundaries.

### Fragmented explanation

Conceptual material is scattered through task or reference pages and never
receives enough space to develop a coherent account.

## Recommendation patterns

Prefer one change that improves the reader's mental model, for example:

- define a clearer conceptual question;
- narrow an overgrown topic;
- reorganize around relationships or causes instead of product components;
- add a missing connection between concepts;
- add context that explains an otherwise isolated fact;
- surface a meaningful alternative or trade-off;
- move a substantial procedure into a how-to guide;
- move a systematic factual catalogue into reference;
- separate an independent conceptual question into its own explanation.

Do not strip explanation down to neutral facts merely for concision.

Its job is to develop understanding through context, connections, reasons,
relationships, and perspective.
